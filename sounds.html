Sounds.

Strange beats synthesized in your browser. Free WAV downloads.

If you are looking for sounds nobody else has, this is the page. Every loop here is built from raw oscillators and noise the moment you press play, in your tab, not fetched from a sample library. Chant hooks, West African drums, sebene guitars, log drum bass, brass fanfares, vinyl crackle. Pick one and it becomes the footer beat on every page; download one and your browser renders the full loop to a WAV file on the spot, free.

  1. Crackle

    84 BPM · lo-fi boom-bap

    The original. Dusty keys over a swung boom-bap kit, a sub on every root, and a vinyl crackle bed the whole way through.

  2. Stride

    126 BPM · deep house

    Four on the floor. A kick every beat, claps on two and four, open hats in the gaps, a filtered bass that only speaks between the kicks, and chords that pump against the drums.

  3. Sprint

    148 BPM · log drum, afro-tech

    The fast one. A broken kick, shakers on every sixteenth, a clave you can clap to, and the bass is a log drum: a sine that bends into each note and bounces. Built to move hips, not heads.

  4. Parade

    132 BPM · brass fanfare and flute

    The upbeat one. A trumpet call opens it, then a marching snare, a tambourine, brass stabs on every chord, and a flute that runs up and down the top the whole way. Major key, hands in the air.

  5. Rumba

    138 BPM · Congolese sebene · for video

    For the dance video. No intro, the sebene starts on the first beat: two clean guitars locked in sixteenths, a lead riff on top, a cowbell, and a bass that skips. Kinshasa at full speed, made to loop under fifteen seconds of footwork.

  6. Bounce

    104 BPM · Afrobeats · for video

    For the dance video. Starts on the hook: a log drum bass line you can hum, shakers on every sixteenth, a lazy clap, a whistle on top, and a crowd “oh” every second bar. Mid-tempo swing, hips first.

  7. Anthem

    92 BPM · rap, whistle and fanfare

    A rap beat whose hook is a chant. Bong is a low brass hit with an 808 under it, bing is a whistle above it: bong bong bong, bong bing bong, bong bing bong, bong bong bong. A whistle answers on the fourth beat and a fanfare turns every fourth bar. Hard snare, slow hats, room to rap.

  8. Anthem II

    96 BPM · rap, the drums of West Africa

    The chant returns, louder, over a drum ensemble: dundun bass drums under every bong, a djembe talking in sixteenths, a talking drum that bends between the calls, and a sabar crack on the snare. Four sections of eight bars, each one heavier than the last, the fanfare at the end.

  9. Rally

    94 BPM · rap, ping pong chant

    A chant, word for word, one syllable per eighth note with a breath at every full stop. Pong is a log drum and a low brass hit, ping is a kalimba and a whistle blip. The phrases are uneven, seven then seventeen, and the drums hold a straight line under them so the chant is the thing that swerves.

  10. Rally II

    94 BPM · rap, big and clean

    The chant again, built to play loud in a car: a few big things and nothing in between. One 808 that glides under the pongs, a low horn on each pong and the same horn an octave up on each ping, a boom on every pong, a snare like a wall, and hats. No melody over it, no effects. Just the beat and the chant, twice through.

For short video

Rumba and Bounce were built for a vertical clip. They skip the intro and land on the hook in the first beat, which is what a viewer decides on. Each loop is 16 bars, under half a minute, so one download covers a whole short with room to cut. Drop the WAV on your timeline, trim to the move, and let the loop point do the work.

Why they sound strange

Nothing here is a recording. Sample packs are recordings of real drums and horns that thousands of producers share. These loops are generated: the kicks, snares, horns, whistles, log drums, djembes and crowd voices are all built from sine, sawtooth and square waves and shaped noise. That is why they sound unusual, and why nobody else has these exact sounds. Two of the beats, Anthem and Rally, started life as a chant typed out word for word, then rendered literally: the low syllable became a horn, the high syllable a whistle.

Questions

Can I use these on TikTok, Reels or YouTube Shorts? Yes. Rumba, Bounce, Anthem and Rally were built for short vertical video: no intro, the hook lands on the first beat, and each file is under a minute and a half.

What format is the download? 16-bit mono WAV at 44.1 kHz, between 4 and 8 megabytes, which every video editor and music app imports.

Is anything uploaded or tracked? No. The loop is rendered inside your browser and handed to you as a file. No server is involved.

Will you make more? Yes. New loops appear here and in the footer picker as they are finished. The generator that builds them can also be pointed at a chant of your own; the contact page is the place to send one.

How the download works

Pressing Download WAV renders one full pass of the loop, about a minute and a half, through an offline copy of the same synth. The result is packed as 16-bit mono WAV at 44.1 kHz and handed to your browser as a file. Nothing is uploaded and no server is involved, so it takes a second or two on a phone and is free to use in your own projects.
